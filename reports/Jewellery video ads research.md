# Keep the pendant still, move the light

Jewellery brands that look premium in short vertical video do two things. They use slow shots with one movement each: light passing over metal, a slow turn, a pendant settling, the piece worn on a person. And they leave out the loud tools of feed advertising: big text, urgency, discounts and hard hooks. For paid ads, the format that performs best in the 2026 data is worn, creator-style footage, while plain studio product shots score lowest. That points a small atelier towards hands, necks and craft, with a clean product shot used as one beat of the ad. For a shop that builds video from approved stills with Higgsfield's image-to-video models, the evidence agrees with the first failed test. These models redraw the piece in every frame. They recompute the lighting, which is why the off-white background drifted to grey. They also invent small parts they cannot see clearly, which is why the bail was redrawn bigger and doubled. Every move of the camera makes this worse. No public test shows any model reliably keeping a jump ring the size of a few pixels. So the recommended plan does not depend on the model getting it right. Camera moves are done in the edit, never by the model. Only the light is allowed to move. The approved still is the first and last frame. The approved pixels of the pendant go back over any AI clip where the bail changes. Every clip is colour-locked back to 251, 251, 253. On that basis a 12–14 s ad is realistic at about **55–80 Higgsfield credits** (roughly €3–4 on the Plus plan), with two of its five shots made in the edit at no credit cost.

## Luxury houses film the craft; paid ads favour worn footage

The clearest picture of how luxury houses use short video comes from agency observation, not from frame-by-frame study. The research tools could not play Instagram or TikTok video, so **no second-by-second breakdown of a Cartier, Tiffany, Van Cleef & Arpels, Bulgari, Pomellato, Messika or Chopard Reel is verified here.** London agency 303 describes the luxury approach as "luxury lo-fi": the brand's codes stay fixed (palette, casting, how the piece is handled, restraint), while pacing and format follow each platform. It suggests four kinds of content. The first is **close-ups of setting, metalwork and finishing, with no narration**. The second is selected behind-the-scenes moments, about "one considered video a fortnight". The third is creators wearing pieces in real life rather than "holding them to camera". The fourth is stories told in episodes. Its Cartier examples fit this: the Jewelry Institute opened for one day to show how a bracelet is made, and *Le Chœur des Pierres* was released in chapters ([303 London](https://www.303.london/blog/how-luxury-jewellery-brands-should-approach-tiktok-without-losing-their-premium-positioning)). Bulgari's Serpenti 75th anniversary shows the other end of the budget scale. It reached **$1.56M Media Impact Value, $694K of it from social in two days**, through events and celebrities, not through Reels made in a studio ([Launchmetrics](https://www.launchmetrics.com/resources/blog/successful-jewelry-digital-campaigns)). Across luxury brands in the first half of 2026, behind-the-scenes access, ambassadors and cinematic reveals did best. The brands posted about 3 times a week on TikTok and 6 on Instagram ([Dash Social](https://www.dashsocial.com/social-media-benchmarks/luxury-industry)).

Premium and direct-to-consumer brands spend their ad money differently. Mejuri ran Spark Ads, which boost posts from the brand's own account and from creators' accounts, together with Search Ads. It reported a **2x conversion rate and 88% more efficient CPA** ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/inspiration/mejuri-search-ads-case-study)). James Allen's Spark Ads reported **+25% CTR and +24% CVR** against its other ads ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/business/ar/inspiration/james-allen-390)). Sistie found that creator and POV formats "significantly outperformed" standard product videos, without giving figures ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/inspiration/sistie-x-dvisionmedia-tiktok-success-stories)). Pandora's "Be Love" campaign measured **+14 points of ad recall** in a brand-lift study ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/it/inspiration/pandora-us-driving-measurable-impact)). All of these are brand-reported case studies published by TikTok, so they are vendor claims. The largest independent dataset found agrees with them, though its scores are a vendor's own index. It covers 8,405 jewellery and accessory ads on Meta in Q1 2026, which scored by asset type as follows: **UGC/organic 45, lifestyle/editorial 41, branded studio 31, product shot 27**. Editorial ads ran longest, with a median of 27 days ([Benly](https://benly.ai/benchmarks/fashion-apparel/jewelry-accessories)). The notes found nothing specific on Missoma, Pomellato, Messika or Chopard. Searches found **nothing at all on Bulgarian jewellers** (ASTO Gold, Aristo, Kirkorian); the owner should check their accounts and the Meta Ad Library by hand.

For Cullinan, the conclusion is that a clean pendant on off-white is the weakest paid format if it stands alone. It is a good opening beat when followed by the piece worn at the neck and by a line about the atelier. The atelier's real asset, hands at the bench in Veliko Tarnovo, is what luxury houses build their organic content around, and filming it costs almost nothing.

## One motion per shot, slow and evenly lit

For a single small piece, practitioners agree on a short list of shots. The first is **a soft highlight travelling across the metal while the piece stays still**. The others are a slow turntable rotation that makes edges catch the light, a rack focus from metal to stone, a pendant swinging gently and settling on its chain, and the piece worn on the body ([Pollo AI](https://pollo.ai/hub/jewelry-video-prompts-product-showcases); [BriteCo](https://brite.co/blog/how-to-shoot-a-jewelry-video/); [cinematography.net](https://cinematography.net/edited-pages/Jewelry-shoot....htm)). The rule that matters most is **"one primary motion and at most one subtle secondary motion"** ([Cliprise](https://www.cliprise.app/learn/workflows/ecommerce/ai-jewelry-product-video-generator-workflow)). Fast moves blur the details, and a turntable must match the backdrop's white or a seam shows ([Rapaport](https://rapaport.com/trade/blog/how-to-take-professional-quality-jewelry-photos-and-videos-yourself-2/); [Pixc](https://pixc.com/blog/jewelry-photography-tips/)). Light should be diffused from at least two sides, because jewellery is "entirely reflective". The common faults that make jewellery look cheap are blown or dull metal, wrong white balance, dust and fingerprints, and cluttered props ([Pixc](https://pixc.com/blog/jewelry-photography-tips/); [Squareshot](https://www.squareshot.com/post/jewelry-product-photography-common-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them)).

On length and the hook, the platforms and the luxury register pull in different directions, and the plan below sits between them. TikTok's own guidance asks for **9:16, the proposition in the first 3 seconds, a hook within 6, sound on, and a call to action at the end**. It recommends no length ([TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en)). Third-party guides settle on **15–30 s for conversion ads, 15–21 s for DTC shops**. These are not official figures ([Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/tiktok-ad-creative-best-practices-2026)). 303 London tells luxury brands to drop loud hooks, text overlays, countdowns, emoji and discount logic, and to use considered sound or silence instead ([303 London](https://www.303.london/blog/how-luxury-jewellery-brands-should-approach-tiktok-without-losing-their-premium-positioning)). The Meta data supports this calmer opening. **62% of jewellery video ads open on "visual intrigue"**, and **53.7% carry discount framing**, so a calm ad led by craft stands apart in the feed ([Benly](https://benly.ai/benchmarks/fashion-apparel/jewelry-accessories)). In practice the strongest glint fills the first second, with no logo. One short Bulgarian line states a buying fact. Sound is a single soft track, and the end card holds the name for 1–2 seconds.

## Benchmarks: hook rate 25% acceptable, 30% good, refresh every 3–4 weeks

Hook rate is 3-second plays divided by impressions. Across 88,329 Meta sales video ads in January–June 2026 it averaged **25.44%**, and **30% or more beats every industry average** ([Billo](https://billo.app/blog/h1-2026-video-ad-benchmarks/)). A hold rate (15-second plays) above 50% is a secondary target ([Billo](https://billo.app/blog/hook-rate-to-hold-rate)). Jewellery's hook effectiveness is reported about **7.9 points below** the cross-industry average. Creatives wear out fast: the median ad lives **24 days**, and none were still running at 90 days ([Benly](https://benly.ai/benchmarks/fashion-apparel/jewelry-accessories)). Luxury brands in the first half of 2026 earned 4.2% engagement on TikTok and 2.5% on Instagram ([Dash Social](https://www.dashsocial.com/social-media-benchmarks/luxury-industry)). For a small luxury pilot, 303 advises judging by **saves, profile visits, branded search and qualified site traffic**, not views. It also advises paying to promote only content that has already done well organically ([303 London](https://www.303.london/blog/how-luxury-jewellery-brands-should-approach-tiktok-without-losing-their-premium-positioning)). No Bulgarian or EU-specific figures for CPM or hook rate were found. For Cullinan this matters now, because the store is private and the theme unpublished. Until the store is live and a payment method works, the video's first use is organic posts on Instagram and Facebook. Those posts test the opening and build the engagement that 303 says should come before any paid budget.

## Why the first Kling test failed, and what AI can and cannot do

The failed test (Kling 3.0 Pro, 5 s, start image only, push-in + orbit + light sweep) broke every rule the sources agree on. It used three motions instead of one. The orbit forced the model to invent a side it had never seen. With no end frame, nothing held the last frames to the approved image. Invideo's account of why jewellery drifts matches both faults: **the model re-creates the piece in every generation, and it recomputes the lighting on reflective surfaces for each render** ([Invideo](https://invideo.io/faq/why-is-jewelry-so-hard-to-keep-consistent-in-ai/)). Hailuo's guide says fast zooms combined with rotation cause warping. It says the words "shiny" and "sparkly" produce noisy pixels, while physical terms ("polished yellow gold", "specular highlights", "consistent studio softbox") stay stable. It recommends **2–3 rerolls per shot** ([Hailuo](https://hailuoai.video/knowledge/ai-video-jewelry-physics-ecommerce-guide)). Kling's own jewellery page says there is no jewellery mode. It warns against fast spins and against moving backgrounds or lighting, and its demo clip is only about 4 s long ([Kling](https://kling.ai/topics/ai-jewelry-video)). A review of Kling 4.0 says there is **no documented guarantee of identical product details**. It advises checking the first, middle and last frames against the approved image, and switching to "controlled animation of the original photo" when the shape keeps changing ([WaveSpeed](https://wavespeed.ai/blog/ai-use-cases/kling-4-0-review/)).

Using the approved still as both the first and last frame is the standard way to hold both ends of a clip. Higgsfield's guide says the start frame sets composition, light and colour. It warns that lighting that differs between the two frames causes flicker, and it says 5 s clips transition most cleanly ([Higgsfield](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/Kling-Start-End-Frames)). Using the same image at both ends makes a seamless loop ([Higgsfield](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/FepbaEyNStzJLJivRIOFg)). It does not protect the frames in between, which is why the camera must stay locked and only the light may move ([Magic Hour](https://magichour.ai/blog/ai-product-video-prompts)). Positive wording ("the camera holds completely still") is recommended over "no camera movement" ([Atlabs](https://www.atlabs.ai/blog/how-to-prompt-with-runway-gen-4-image-to-video-a-comprehensive-guide)). Shots involving hands or worn pieces are not tested by any source, and the Hailuo guide explicitly leaves them out. **The only way to guarantee the design never changes is to keep the piece's real pixels.** In that approach, the model generates only the scene and the light, and the approved still is composited back on top ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/make-a-jewelry-product-video)). A filter applied over a whole composite can change the product's colour, so effects should be masked to the piece ([Pippit](https://www.pippit.ai/blog/match-new-background-to-product-lighting)).

## The plan: a 13-second ad built from the approved stills

The ad has five shots. Two are made from the approved stills alone, with no AI and no cost: the camera move is done in the edit, and any AI clip that touches the pendant is guarded with the approved pixels. The pendant is a gold four-leaf clover with a white-gold starfish. The yellow 01 still and the neck photos are approved, on a 251, 251, 253 background. Bulgarian lines use only the permitted facts. **Confirm with the owner whether this pendant is 14K or 18K before the text is set**, and never say a chain is included.

Do not use Kling 3.0 4K (30) or Seedance standard 1080p (60) for this. Their extra sharpness does not stop the redraw, and shots 1, 4 and 5 already carry the 4K detail from the still. Sound comes from a commercially licensed library track (for example the platforms' own commercial music libraries) or silence. Audio generated with the clip is turned off.

## Conclusion

The failed test does not show that AI video is unusable for jewellery. It shows that the model was asked to do the one thing it cannot be trusted with: decide what the piece looks like while the camera moves. The useful division of labour turns that around. The approved photograph decides the piece, the edit decides the camera, and the model contributes only what a still cannot give, which is moving light and a living neck. This is also cheaper, because the shots that carry the hook and the end card cost nothing. It brings a strategic advantage too. The benchmark data say studio product shots are the weakest paid format, and the luxury guidance says craft and worn pieces carry the brand. So this AI-assisted ad is best treated as the first entry in a series, while hands at the atelier bench and customers wearing the pieces are the content most likely to justify paid budget later. The largest open question is not which model to use. It is whether any of them can keep a three-millimetre bail through five seconds. The matched four-model test answers that question for 37.5 credits, and the guard-and-composite step makes the answer matter less.
